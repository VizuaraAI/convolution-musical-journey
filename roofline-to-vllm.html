§ I

A roofline, and the first inference.

Every inference workload lives somewhere on a roofline plot. Where it lives is the single most important fact about it.

The roofline is a simple picture. The horizontal axis is arithmetic intensity — how many FLOPs the kernel performs per byte of memory it reads. The vertical axis is achieved performance, in FLOPs per second. The plot has two ceilings: a flat ceiling on the right (peak compute), and a sloped ceiling on the left (peak memory bandwidth). Where these meet is the ridge. To the left of it, the kernel is memory-bound. To the right, compute-bound.

Why does decode sit so far left? Because it is autoregressive. Generating one new token requires reading the model's parameters once, multiplying them by a single token's worth of activation, and writing one new token out. Lots of bytes read, very few FLOPs done with them. Arithmetic intensity is tiny.

Prefill is the opposite. The whole prompt is processed at once, so the same parameters get reused across many tokens. Many FLOPs per byte. The dot lives near the ridge.

This asymmetry is the entire reason inference is hard. Training looks like prefill: massive parallelism, the dot is near the ridge, the GPU is happy. Inference looks like decode: a thin trickle of work, the GPU is starving. Every chapter from here on is a way to make decode look more like prefill on the roofline.

Roofline is from Williams, Waterman & Patterson (2009). It was originally about classical scientific kernels. It turns out to describe LLM inference more cleanly than almost any model we have invented since.

§ II

The KV cache, the rescuer.

Naive autoregressive decode does an enormous amount of duplicated work. The KV cache is the realization that you can stop doing it.

Without the cache, generating token t requires recomputing the keys and values for all previous tokens — every single one of t-1, t-2, … 1 — even though those K and V vectors did not change since the last step. The total work over a sequence of length N is O(N²) matmuls. Most of it is redundant.

The cache is the obvious fix. After computing each new token's K and V, store them. At step t, only one new token's K and V are computed; the rest are read from cache. Per-step work drops from O(N) to O(1). Total work over the sequence drops from O(N²) to O(N).

naive total  ∝  N² · d² · L    →    cached total  ∝  N · d² · L

On the roofline, the cache moves the decode dot a meaningful step toward the ridge. We are doing fewer FLOPs per token, but we are also reading a lot fewer bytes — we do not re-read all the past tokens any more, just the small KV cache slab. Net result: arithmetic intensity climbs, the dot lifts.

The KV cache is the first chapter where the GPU stops being starved. It is also where it becomes claustrophobic.
§ III

The same hero, the new villain.

The KV cache fixes one problem and creates another. Speed is up, but memory now grows linearly with every token in every active session.

The per-token cache is exactly as the book derives it in Chapter 7:

cache bytes per token  =  4 · H · D · L

Two bytes for K, two for V (FP16), times H heads, D head dimension, L layers. Plug in Llama-3-70B-style numbers — H = 64, D = 128, L = 80 — and you get ~2.6 MB per token. At a 32K-token context: ~84 GB per user. An H100 has 80 GB total. One user fills the GPU before any compute happens.

On the roofline, the cache also pulls the decode dot back to the left. Every step now reads more bytes — the entire cache, all of it, every time. Arithmetic intensity sinks. The compute is small, the bytes are huge, and the dot is once again deep on the bandwidth ramp.

Two bottlenecks now. Memory capacity (you literally run out of space) and memory bandwidth (the cache is read once per step, dominating the byte budget). Both are the same villain wearing two costumes. Both are the bytes-per-token formula above.

The cache formula has four factors. Three are fixed by the model architecture: H, D, L. The interesting one is N — and N grows every time someone sends a longer prompt. This is why long-context inference is hard.

§ IV

Lifting the roofline by shrinking bytes.

If the bytes are the problem, the bytes are the lever. Shrink them, and arithmetic intensity climbs by exactly the same factor.

The decode dot's horizontal position on the roofline is

arithmetic intensity  =  FLOPs  /  bytes read

FLOPs are mostly fixed by the model. The thing we can move is the denominator. Cut the bytes by 4× and the dot moves 4× to the right. Cut them by 64× and the dot can reach the ridge.

There are exactly two terms in the per-token cache formula 4·H·D·L that we can attack architecturally. H, the number of K/V heads, and the implicit N factor that multiplies the per-token cost into a per-session cost. The book calls these two attacks compressing across heads (Chapter 8) and compressing across tokens (Chapter 9). Together they are the two scissors that cut the cache down to size.

§ V

Across heads — MHA, MQA, GQA, MLA.

The first lever. Every variant in this section keeps softmax attention over the full sequence, but stores fewer K/V projections per token.

Reducing H in the cache formula is architectural. You either pretrain with the smaller-K/V design from scratch, or you uptrain an MHA checkpoint with a few percent of the original tokens. Inference-side, the win is direct: cache bytes drop by exactly the factor you compressed by.

The MLA absorption trick.

MLA's elegance is in one algebraic identity. Instead of storing per-head K, the model stores a small latent c_KV ∈ ℝ^R per token. At decode, the score for query q against past keys becomes

Q · Kᵀ  =  (x · W_Q) · (c_KV · W_UK)ᵀ  =  x · (W_Q · W_UKᵀ) · c_KVᵀ

The bracketed product is a constant matrix that can be precomputed once at model load and absorbed into W_Q. After that, the kernel only ever multiplies x by a single fused weight matrix and dots into the cached latent. Full per-head K is never materialized at decode time. Cache stays small; per-head diversity stays full.

DeepSeek-V3 ships R = 512 over L = 61 layers. Per-token cache: 2·512·61 ≈ 62 KB, versus ~4 MB for an equivalent MHA. ~64× compression, no measurable quality cost on standard evals.

§ VI

Across tokens — sliding window, linear attention, Mamba.

The second lever. Where head compression shrinks H, token compression bounds — or eliminates — the N factor that makes the cache grow with sequence length.

Three families, in order of how aggressively they compress:

Sliding window — a circular buffer.

Each token attends only to the last W keys. The cache becomes a circular buffer of fixed size W; older slots are overwritten as new tokens arrive. The cache no longer grows with N at all — it caps at 4·W·H·D·L bytes and stays there forever.

Linear attention — a constant-size running state.

Sliding window keeps softmax but throws away old tokens. Linear attention goes the other way — keep all tokens, but throw away the softmax. The trick is associativity:

softmax(QKᵀ) V  (can't reorder)   →  φ(Q) · ( φ(K)ᵀ V )  (can)

With the softmax replaced by a kernel feature map φ, the inner product φ(K)ᵀV becomes a small D × D matrix that is independent of N. Past tokens are accumulated into this matrix incrementally:

St  =  St−1  +  φ(Kt)ᵀ Vt    (D × D, no N)

Mamba — selectivity to the rescue.

The fix to the context bottleneck is the recognition that the model should be allowed to choose what to remember. In linear attention every input is folded into the state with the same weights. Mamba, the selective state-space model, makes the recurrence's parameters input-dependent: the matrices A, B, and the timestep Δ are functions of the current token. A token that the model has decided is important "passes through" with full strength; a token that is filler is suppressed.

Mamba ships with a hardware-aware selective-scan kernel that is essential — without it, the input-dependent recurrence is too slow on a GPU. The kernel is the reason Mamba is competitive in practice.

§ VII

The three axes.

Quality, memory, and throughput are not on the same line. Every variant we have met is a point in a triangle, and the right choice depends on which corner you care about.

§ VIII

In reality, models mix everything.

No production model picks one variant and stops. Real systems combine head compression, token compression, and a battery of systems-level tricks. The architecture is a stack, not a choice.

Two patterns to notice. First, every architecture combines a head choice and a token choice — they are orthogonal levers. Second, the kernel column is invariant. FlashAttention is the substrate. It is not one of the choices in the menu; it is the floor of the building.

§ IX

FlashAttention — the layer beneath all of it.

FlashAttention is not a competitor to GQA, MLA, or Mamba. It sits underneath all of them. It computes whatever attention math you have decided on, but it computes it without ever writing the giant intermediate matrix to slow memory.

To understand the trick, picture the GPU's memory hierarchy. HBM is large and slow (~3 TB/s on H100, but cents per nanosecond compared to the speed of compute). On-chip SRAM is small and fast (~228 KB per streaming multiprocessor, roughly two orders of magnitude faster). The standard attention kernel materializes the N × N score matrix in HBM, reads it back to apply softmax, then reads it again to multiply by V. The matrix is the bottleneck.

The "online softmax" trick is what makes this work. A naive softmax needs to scan all logits twice — once to find the max for numerical stability, once to exponentiate and normalize. FlashAttention rewrites it as a streaming reduction: as each new key tile arrives, the running maximum, the running denominator, and the running output are updated in a single pass. The math is identical to a standard softmax. The schedule is unrecognizable.

same output as MHA  ·  tile loop  ·  online softmax  ·  SRAM-resident
§ X

Why N²·d²/M is the result that matters.

Both kernels do the same FLOPs. What differs is the bytes they move between HBM and the chip. The book's Chapter 10 gives the formula in one line — and it is the line that explains everything.

Standard attention does O(N·d) work for the matmuls and O(N²) for the score matrix. Its HBM traffic is dominated by reading and writing that N × N matrix:

standard HBM traffic  =  O(N · d + N²)

FlashAttention never writes the score matrix. Its HBM traffic is dominated by reading and re-reading Q, K, V tiles in a way set by SRAM size M:

FlashAttention HBM traffic  =  O( N² · d² / M )

The interpretation: d² is fixed by the model architecture and is small (16K floats for a typical head). M is the SRAM, and on a modern GPU it's tens of thousands of floats per SM. The ratio d²/M is comfortably under 1, and FlashAttention's HBM traffic ends up quadratic in N but with a tiny coefficient, while standard attention's traffic is quadratic in N with a coefficient of 1. At long context, that small coefficient is what saves you.

FlashAttention does not change what you compute. It changes which memory the bytes of the intermediate result ever touch.
§ XI

Today's lecture — inside vLLM.

Every chapter of this story has been about the math and the kernel. The next layer up is the engine: the runtime that takes all of these techniques and serves them to many users at once.

By now you have a model with GQA or MLA, a token-compression scheme if you wanted one, and a FlashAttention kernel underneath. What you do not yet have is anything that turns a flow of HTTP requests into a flow of tokens — that handles dynamic batching, fairness, prefix sharing, page-table-style cache allocation, and the dozens of decisions per second that determine whether your H100 is at 30% utilization or 90%.

That is what an inference engine does. The most important open-source one is vLLM. It is the subject of today's class.

Today on the whiteboard

vLLM — the engine that wraps the kernel.

The runtime that takes everything in this essay and serves it. PagedAttention turns the KV cache into an OS-style page table. Continuous batching schedules at the iteration boundary, not the request boundary. Prefix caching shares the cost of repeated system prompts. Chunked prefill mixes long-prefill and short-decode work without head-of-line blocking.

Each of those is a chapter unto itself in the book. Each one is the engine answering a question that this essay has been quietly setting up. The KV cache is small now, thanks to MLA or GQA — how do we allocate it across users? The decode dot is closer to the ridge — how do we keep it there when prefill arrives mid-conversation? The kernel is fast — how do we feed it without bubbles?
