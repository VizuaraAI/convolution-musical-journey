A practical map across the attention chapters

Which attention
variants need
retraining?

Not every "attention speedup" is the same kind of object. Some rearrange the GPU schedule and leave the math untouched. Others change the architecture itself — fewer K/V heads, a windowed mask, no softmax — and the model has to be (re)trained to use them. This essay sorts the entire family and tells you exactly what to spin up to reproduce each variant yourself.

The single test
attention(Q,K,V)  ≡  attention'(Q,K,V)  ?
if yes → systems trick (no retrain).   if no → architecture change (retrain).

This walkthrough sits across three chapters of the book. Ch 8 compresses the cache across heads (MHA → MQA → GQA → MLA). Ch 9 compresses it across tokens (sliding window, linear attention, SSMs, Mamba). Ch 10 is FlashAttention — a systems-level rearrangement of the same math. The training cost of adopting each one is wildly different, and that difference is what this page draws.

§ I

Two kinds of object, one shared name.

It's tempting to read Chapters 8, 9, and 10 of the book as a single shelf of "attention variants." The terminology hides a clean line that runs straight through the family.

Every "faster attention" technique you have read about is one of two very different kinds of object. The first kind keeps the operator's input-output relation identical and just computes it differently. The second kind defines a new operator — different parameter count, different mask, different softmax. The first costs nothing to adopt at inference. The second costs, at minimum, a fine-tune — and often a full pretraining run.

Calling them all "attention variants" hides this distinction. The job of this essay is to make it surgical, and to tell you what each side of the line costs to reproduce.

A faster route to the same destination is free. A faster route to a different destination demands you re-teach the model where it's going.
§ II

One axis, three buckets.

Pin every attention variant to a single axis: does it produce the same output as standard multi-head attention, given the same trained weights?

Three buckets, then:

① Systems-level (Ch 10). Same output to within numerical precision. You can take a checkpoint trained with vanilla attention and run inference with FlashAttention or PagedAttention. Loss does not move. Accuracy does not move. Tokens out are the same.

② Uptraining (Ch 8 — across heads). The architecture changes — fewer K/V heads, or a latent compression — but the model still does softmax attention over the full sequence. You can recover the lost behaviour cheaply by continuing pretraining for a small fraction of the original token budget. Ainslie et al. (2023) reported ~5% for GQA.

③ Full retrain (Ch 9 — across tokens). The mask, the receptive field, or the softmax itself changes. The model has to learn how to use the new operator from scratch. There is no checkpoint-laundering trick that lets you skip this in general.

"Retraining" in this essay means continued pretraining with the new operator. Task-level fine-tuning is too short and downstream to fix an operator change.

§ III

FlashAttention — same math, smarter schedule.

This is Chapter 10 in one line. FlashAttention is the canonical systems-level optimization: the output is bit-equal (modulo float order-of-operations) to standard attention. It reorders where the computation happens, not what is computed.

Standard attention computes a full N × N score matrix S = QKᵀ/√d, applies softmax row-wise, then multiplies by V. The score matrix is a memory hog — for a 32K context it is a billion floats, and it lives on slow HBM. FlashAttention never materializes it. Instead it tiles Q, K, V into blocks that fit in fast SRAM and computes the softmax incrementally using a streaming algorithm.

Because the operator is the same, you do not need a single new gradient step. Every Llama, Mistral, Qwen, GPT checkpoint trained with eager attention works with FlashAttention at inference time. PyTorch's scaled_dot_product_attention already swaps in FlashAttention 2 when the shapes allow it. You change a runtime flag, not a weight.

FA1 / FA2 / FA3 differ in how cleverly the schedule overlaps memory movement and matmul on different GPUs (A100 vs H100), but the math contract is the same.

A subtle point: floating-point addition is non-associative. FA outputs are not literally bit-equal to a naive implementation; they're numerically equivalent within rounding. This never matters for downstream task accuracy.

§ IV

Compressing across heads — MQA, GQA, MLA.

This is Chapter 8 of the book in three architectures. Every variant in this section keeps softmax attention over the full sequence, but reduces the K/V footprint. The math is now genuinely different from MHA — but the change is small enough to be cheap to undo with continued pretraining.

In MHA with H heads, the per-token KV cache is 4·H·D·L bytes. For Llama-3-70B at H = 64, D = 128, L = 80 that is 2.6 MB per token, or 84 GB at 32K context — completely impractical. Almost every modern open model attacks the H factor first, because H is the only term you can shrink architecturally without changing the operator's structure.

Two consequences. First: the parameter count drops, so a model trained with full MHA has no equivalent set of weights to "lift" into MQA. Second: the inductive bias changes — heads that previously specialized in different K subspaces now share. Naive weight averaging from MHA → GQA gets you a head start, but the model is degraded until it is given more training signal.

The good news is that this gap closes fast. The GQA paper (Ainslie et al. 2023) found that ~5% of original pretraining compute recovered nearly all of the MHA quality, with the inference advantages of GQA fully retained. That's the cell where "uptraining" lives: a real architectural change, but the cost of learning the new operator is only a sliver of the cost of learning the language in the first place.

cost(MHA → GQA)   ≈   0.05 · cost(pretraining)

Llama-3 (all sizes, G = 8), Qwen-2/3 (G = 4), Mistral, and Command-R all ship GQA. They were either pretrained with GQA from scratch or uptrained from an MHA cousin.

MLA — head compression done properly.

The most sophisticated head-compression in production is DeepSeek-V3's Multi-head Latent Attention. Instead of dropping K/V heads, MLA down-projects each token to a small latent vector c_KV ∈ ℝ^R (typically R = 512) and caches only the latent. At attention time, an absorption trick merges the up-projection into W_Q so K and V are never reconstructed in the kernel. Per-head diversity is preserved; the cache shrinks by ~64× relative to MHA at no measurable quality cost.

cache_MLA  =  2 · R · L  bytes per token   (vs 4 · H · D · L for MHA)

MLA is still architectural — the model has the down/up-projections baked into its weights — so you cannot lift an MHA checkpoint into MLA at runtime. But like GQA, the operator is still softmax attention over the full sequence, and the conversion is recoverable with continued pretraining. DeepSeek pretrained from scratch with MLA; for an existing checkpoint, the GQA-style uptraining recipe is the correct mental model.

§ V

Compressing across tokens — sliding window and sparse.

This is the first half of Chapter 9. Where head compression shrunk the H factor, token compression attacks the N factor — the cache no longer grows linearly with sequence length, because there are positions the model can no longer see at all.

Standard attention is dense: every query attends to every preceding key. Sliding-window attention restricts each query to the last W keys, and the per-token cache becomes 4·W·H·D·L — constant in N. Sparse attention (block-local + a few global tokens) similarly drops the dependence on N. Either way, the function the model is computing is no longer the same function: information about tokens outside the mask is gone.

Sliding-window attention has a partial escape route. Because each layer attends to its own window, deep stacks build up a larger effective receptive field — roughly W × L. Mistral-7B (W = 4K, L = 32) reaches an effective receptive field of ~128K tokens through layer stacking, and the KV cache becomes a circular buffer of fixed size W. But Mistral was pretrained with the windowed mask from scratch. You cannot take a dense Llama checkpoint, switch on a window, and expect anything good — direct attention is strictly stronger than indirect-through-layers attention, and the model has to learn how to route information through the relay.

Sparse attention (Longformer, BigBird, DeepSeek's NSA) is similar. The model has to learn that certain tokens are "global summary" tokens and that block-local attention is enough for the rest. That is a learned skill, not a free schedule swap. Even Gemma-2/3's hybrid pattern (every fifth layer is full attention) was trained that way from the start.

A nuance: short fine-tunes on a frozen backbone with a small windowed extension can work for some downstream tasks. But the underlying language modelling capability degrades unless you continue pretraining with the new mask.

§ VI

Linear attention, SSMs, and Mamba.

The second half of Chapter 9. Sliding window kept softmax but capped the receptive field at W. This family takes the more radical step: drop the softmax entirely so the entire past collapses into a fixed-size running state.

Standard softmax attention computes softmax(QKᵀ)V — the row-wise softmax forces the N×N score matrix to materialize. Linear attention replaces this with φ(Q) · (φ(K)ᵀ V) for a kernel feature map φ. The inner product is now D×D instead of N×N, and the entire past is summarized by a constant-size running state S = Σ φ(K_t)ᵀ V_t. The cache no longer grows with N at all.

Because softmax is what gives attention its sharp, query-dependent retrieval behaviour, removing it is not a free swap. Models trained from scratch with linear attention can match dense softmax attention on many benchmarks but typically lose ground on tasks that require precise long-range lookup (the proverbial "needle in a haystack"). RetNet, Mamba, RWKV, and similar architectures are essentially different model families, not patches to existing ones.

One escape route: linearization. Recent work (e.g., Mamba-in-Llama, attention distillation) shows you can take a softmax-attention checkpoint, swap in linear attention layers, and fine-tune to recover most of the original quality. But "fine-tune" here means tens of billions of tokens for a 7B model — well beyond what any task fine-tune costs.

§ VIII

Running the comparison yourself.

Knowing the verdict table is one thing. Running the comparison yourself — head-to-head, same data, same conditions — is what turns the table into a calibration in your head. So: what model size and dataset are large enough to give a real signal, but small enough to fit on a sane budget?

The honest answer: there is a sweet spot around 125M to 410M parameters, trained on 5–20 billion tokens of FineWeb-Edu or SlimPajama. Below this the numbers are too noisy to distinguish architectural choices from seed variance. Above this the reproducibility budget collapses.

This sizing is roughly Pythia-160M's footprint. It is large enough to show meaningful perplexity gaps between architectures and small enough to run a clean grid in a day. Pythia, GPT-Neo-125M, TinyLlama-1.1B, and SmolLM-135M are all reasonable starting points; nanoGPT is the simplest scaffold to modify.

Model

  • Decoder-only Transformer, 12 layers, 12 heads, d_model = 768 → ≈160M params.
  • RoPE positional encoding (so window/sparse variants behave the way modern papers test them).
  • Standard init: GPT-2-style, no exotic tricks.

Data

  • FineWeb-Edu (10B-token subset) — clean, well-curated web text.
  • Or SlimPajama-627B sampled to 10B if you want a more diverse mix.
  • Use a single shared tokenizer (e.g. Llama-2 32K BPE) so perplexity is comparable.

Training budget per variant

  • 10B tokens · context 2048 · batch 0.5M tokens · ≈20K steps.
  • AdamW, cosine schedule, peak LR 6e-4, warmup 1%, weight decay 0.1.
  • Single H100 (or 8× A10G) → ~6 hours per run. Three seeds per variant for noise control.

Variants to compare

  • baseline — dense MHA + FlashAttention (controls for kernel speed).
  • gqa-2, gqa-4, mqa — vary the K/V group count (Ch 8).
  • mla-128 — latent rank 128, the cheapest serious MLA test (Ch 8).
  • swa-256, swa-1024 — sliding window with two widths (Ch 9).
  • block-sparse — block-local + small global token set (Ch 9).
  • linear-elu — linear attention with an ELU+1 feature map (Ch 9).

Evaluation

  • Validation perplexity on a held-out FineWeb-Edu shard (the headline number).
  • HellaSwag, ARC-easy, LAMBADA at this scale — they're noisy but track real capability.
  • Decode throughput at N = 1, 4, 32 batch and 4K, 16K context to expose the systems gap.
  • KV-cache memory per token — the actual reason MQA/GQA exist.

What good looks like

  • FlashAttention swap: identical loss curve, real wall-clock speedup.
  • GQA-4 vs MHA: ≤0.05 perplexity gap, 2–4× smaller KV cache.
  • SWA-1024: small perplexity hit at this scale, big throughput win at long context.
  • Linear attention: visible perplexity gap; exists to be a baseline you can beat.

If you have to pick a single configuration: 160M params, 10B tokens of FineWeb-Edu, three seeds, one H100, six hours per ablation. That gives a believable grid in under a week and is exactly the regime where most modern attention papers do their controlled ablations before scaling up. The published claims about GQA, sliding window, MLA, and linear attention were all initially validated at roughly this scale.

§ X

Closing.

Sharpen the line that runs through Chapters 8, 9, and 10 and you have the durable rule.

FlashAttention (Ch 10) is the textbook case of a systems optimization: the math is identical, the schedule is smarter, no retraining required. Compressing across heads (Ch 8) — MQA, GQA, MLA — is an architecture change but a forgiving one; uptraining on a sliver of the original token budget is enough. Compressing across tokens (Ch 9) — sliding window, sparse, linear, SSMs, Mamba — are operator-level changes that ask the model to learn how to compute attention in a new way, and you owe them a pretraining run.

The reproduction recipe — 160M params, 10B FineWeb-Edu tokens, one H100 — is small enough that the entire grid fits in a week of cloud spend. Most of the published claims in the attention literature were validated at exactly this scale before being scaled up. There is no good reason for any of these architectural decisions to feel mysterious.

Pick a variant. Train it. Watch the loss curve. The picture this essay drew will become a calibration in your head — and you'll never again confuse a kernel rewrite with a pretraining decision.
