§ I

A model is a guesser of next tokens.

Forget — for a moment — everything you've heard about transformers, attention, billions of parameters. Strip the model down to the only thing it actually does at inference time.

A language model, at its irreducible core, plays a single game over and over: given some text so far, predict what comes next. Not a sentence. Not a paragraph. Just the next single piece — the next token.

Show it "The cat sat on the". The model does not "know" the answer. It produces a probability over every possible token in its vocabulary — a long ranked list. mat might get 41%. floor 12%. roof 4%. banana, mercifully, 0.001%.

That ranked list is the model's belief about the world, frozen at this exact moment, given this exact prefix. Everything we are about to do — every formula, every metric — is just a different lens for asking the same question:

When the truth is revealed, was the model's bet a wise one?
§ II

Tokens, and the probability they live in.

A "token" is not a word. A token is whatever your tokenizer says it is — a chunk of text that the model treats as a single atom.

For GPT-style models, the word "unbelievable" might split into three tokens: un · believ · able. The word "the" is one token. A space matters. Punctuation matters. A typo can fragment a word into a long string of tiny pieces.

This matters for perplexity for one specific reason: perplexity is computed per token. Two models with different tokenizers, scored on the same English sentence, are not directly comparable. Always check the tokenizer.

The probability simplex

Whenever the model sees a context, it outputs a vector of length |V| (the vocabulary size — typically 30,000 to 200,000 entries) where every entry is non-negative and the entire vector sums to 1.

§ III

Surprise, measured in bits.

If we want to grade the model, we need a number that is large when the model is wrong and small when the model is right. The most natural such number is surprise.

Imagine I tell you the sun rose this morning. You are not surprised. The probability you assigned to that event was essentially 1. Now imagine I tell you a meteor landed in your kitchen. You are extremely surprised — the probability you'd have assigned to that was nearly 0.

So surprise is some function of probability that goes to 0 when P → 1 and to infinity when P → 0. Many functions could do this, but one specific choice — the negative log probability — has properties so beautiful that information theory was built on it.

surprise(x) = − log P(x)

Why this exact formula? Three reasons, each of which is the entire reason we use it.

Why this function and not another?

① It has a unit: bits.

If P = 1/2, the surprise is exactly 1 bit — the information needed to answer one yes/no question. P = 1/256? That's 8 bits, the size of a byte. Surprise becomes a quantity you can store.

② It is additive across independent events.

If two independent things both happen, their probabilities multiply (P(A,B) = P(A)·P(B)) but their surprises add: −log P(A,B) = −log P(A) + −log P(B). Surprise behaves like a budget.

③ It punishes overconfidence catastrophically.

If your model says "this token has probability 0.0001" and that token is what actually appears next, you are charged −log₂(0.0001) ≈ 13.3 bits of surprise — a lot. If your model said "0", you would be charged infinity. This is exactly the right behaviour: a model that confidently rules out the truth deserves an infinite penalty.

Throughout this essay we use the natural log (base e) when doing math, because the perplexity formula uses exp. Conceptually, base-2 logs (bits) are easier to feel. The ratio between them is just a constant — they mean the same thing.

§ IV

From one token to a sentence.

A model doesn't predict one token in isolation. It predicts an entire stream — token after token, each one conditioned on what came before. To grade it on a sentence, we average its surprise.

Take the sentence "the cat sat on the mat". Tokenized — let's pretend each word is one token — it has 6 tokens. As we slide through it left-to-right, the model produces 6 probability distributions, one per position:

That last line is the famous cross-entropy loss — the loss function used to train every modern language model. It is, mechanically, an average of negative log-probabilities. Conceptually, it is the average surprise per token.

L = − 1N Σi=1N log P(xi | x<i)

Read aloud: "negative one-over-N, sum over all positions, of the log probability the model gave to the actual next token at that position." Every term in this formula is now familiar.

This is the number that appears on every loss curve in every transformer paper ever published. When you see GPT-2 reach a training loss of 3.0, that means: on average, every token in the validation set cost the model 3.0 nats of surprise.

§ V

The exponential trick.

Cross-entropy is mathematically clean — but it speaks in nats, an abstract unit. Researchers wanted a number whose units felt like something you could see. So they exponentiated. The result is perplexity.

Here's the move. We have an average log-probability. If we exponentiate it, we get back to a probability. And one over that probability has a beautiful interpretation: the effective number of equally-likely choices the model is hesitating between.

Perplexity = exp(L) = exp(− 1N Σ log P(xi | x<i))

To see why, suppose the model is perfectly uniform over a vocabulary of size k. Every token gets probability 1/k. Surprise per token is log(k). Exponentiating gets you back to k. So a model that's totally guessing among k tokens has perplexity k. Perplexity is the size of the implicit "guess pool."

The same number, three voices

Perplexity, cross-entropy, and average log-probability all carry the same information; they just speak different dialects.

average log-prob:
ℓ = (1/N) Σ log P(xi|x<i)
cross-entropy (loss):
L = −ℓ
perplexity:
PPL = exp(L) = exp(−ℓ)

If you train a model and watch its loss drop from 4.0 to 3.0, you have just watched its perplexity drop from e⁴ ≈ 54.6 to e³ ≈ 20.1. The model's effective "guess pool" has nearly halved.

A loss of 3.0 sounds abstract. A perplexity of 20 sounds like something. That is the entire reason the unit exists.
§ VI

Perplexity, finally — a number with a soul.

Now the formula has nowhere left to hide. Every symbol means something. Let's read it left to right and feel each piece.

An equivalent and equally lovely form

Take that formula and untangle the algebra:

PPL = exp(− 1N Σ log Pi) = (P1 · P2 · ⋯ · PN)−1/N

Read aloud: perplexity is the inverse of the geometric mean of the probabilities the model assigned to the actual tokens. The geometric mean punishes outliers — even one near-zero probability tanks the whole product. This is exactly why a single confidently-wrong prediction can wreck a model's perplexity score.

Geometric mean instead of arithmetic mean: probabilities multiply, so the average of their contributions must be multiplicative too. Logs let us add; exponentiation undoes it.

§ VIII

What the number does — and doesn't — say.

Perplexity is one of the most widely-cited LM metrics, and one of the most widely-misused. Here's a sober view of when to trust it, and when to look elsewhere.

1perfect oracle (P=1 always)
≈ 30strong LLM on Wikipedia
≈ 50Kuntrained model · vocab size
∞model gives P=0 to any truth

What perplexity gets right

Perplexity is the right metric for one specific question: how well does this model compress text from this distribution? A lower perplexity literally means fewer bits-per-token are needed to encode the corpus under the model's distribution. This is why perplexity is the gold-standard training objective and a beautifully principled scaling-law metric.

What perplexity gets wrong

Tokenizers ruin comparisons.

A model with a finer tokenizer will, all else equal, have a higher per-token perplexity — it's spreading the same surprise across more tokens. Always compare bits-per-character or bits-per-byte instead of raw perplexity across different tokenizers.

It only measures local fluency.

Perplexity is averaged over single-token predictions. A model can have wonderful perplexity and still hallucinate facts, contradict itself across paragraphs, or fail at any task that requires reasoning. Perplexity grades the words, not the thoughts.

It is sensitive to the test corpus.

Perplexity on Wikipedia, on Reddit, on Python code, and on poetry are four different numbers. A model that's "better" by Wikipedia perplexity can be worse by code perplexity. Always cite the corpus.

It ignores calibration beyond the truth.

Perplexity only looks at the probability of the token that actually appeared. A model could be giving 99% to "mat" and 1% to "banana," or 99% to "mat" and 1% to "floor" — same perplexity, very different reasonability.

· · ·

Putting it all together

Perplexity is — at last — the answer to the question we started with: how surprised is your model? It compresses, into one number, the average size of the "guess pool" the model was hesitating between, over every token of every sentence you scored it on.

Lower is better. A perplexity of 1 means perfect prediction. A perplexity of |V| means total ignorance. Everything in between is a measurement of how much of the structure of language your model has internalized.

A model's perplexity is the size of the room it is still in the dark about.

Now read the formula one last time. Every symbol should feel inevitable.

PPL = exp(− 1N Σ log P(xi | x<i))
